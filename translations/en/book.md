# Preface: Talk Things Through Far Enough to Move Forward

Some things are hard to say, and not because we do not know how to put a sentence together.

You want a colleague to send the materials earlier, but worry about sounding relentless. You want to redistribute household chores, but the conversation turns into an accounting of who has contributed more. You explain something for ten minutes, yet someone keeps asking a question you thought you had already answered. At the end of a conversation, both people feel they have been clear. The next day, they act on different understandings.

This book is about those moments. It does not teach you to win every argument or require introverted people to become eloquent speakers. What we will practise is bringing what happened, what we want, and what the other person can or cannot do into a form each person can respond to.

Clarity does not mean agreement. Someone may understand and still refuse. Someone may agree with the direction but have no time to help. Someone may agree and later change their mind. A useful conversation may leave us with an arrangement, a clear disagreement, or the knowledge that we should stop waiting and make other plans. Distinguishing these outcomes is more reliable than getting a vague “Anything is fine.”

The book has twelve chapters, organised by task. You can begin with whatever you are facing: making requests, dividing work, explaining misunderstandings, raising objections, holding meetings, sending written messages, discussing family arrangements, dealing with service problems, navigating power differences, or deciding what to do when agreement is out of reach. Each chapter includes fully developed situations and exchanges. These are not model answers to memorise. A character says a particular thing because of the information they have, the responsibilities they carry, and the limits on their time and resources. When the conditions change, the wording should change too.

The stories, names, organisations, and dialogue are all original fiction, not accounts of the author's experiences or records of real cases. Stories help us see choices; they cannot establish universal laws of human nature. Research and methodological sources appear at the end, distinguishing research findings, proposed methods, and this book's judgments about their application. The book draws inspiration from several approaches, including Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication, but does not rewrite another book chapter by chapter or treat any sentence pattern as a universal key.

Some communication difficulties arise from power, institutions, and conflicting interests, rather than from how well someone speaks. When there are threats or real danger, protecting yourself does not require first passing a test in “acceptable expression.” This book discusses everyday cooperation. It is not a substitute for professional help suited to particular circumstances.

You do not need to find someone to practise with immediately. You might start with a sheet of paper and a recent matter you did not manage to discuss clearly. What, exactly, did I want the other person to answer? What did they actually answer? Did either of us confuse “hearing,” “agreeing,” and “committing to act”?

Start with that difference, and there is already something you can change.

# Chapter 1: Before You Speak, Decide What This Conversation Is About

On Wednesday afternoon, there were only two days left to set up a community photography exhibition. Xu Ning, who was coordinating the volunteers, called Chen He into the corridor. “We need to talk about how we've been working together lately.”

Chen He had just finished moving display stands and still had dust on his hands. “What's gone wrong?” he asked.

“It's not one particular thing. You've been so passive lately.”

“I revised the posters and moved the stands. How much more initiative am I supposed to show?”

Xu Ning had meant to ask whether Chen He could be there on Thursday to receive the printed photographs. Ten minutes later, they were discussing who had been slow to answer messages and who had failed to give advance notice over the previous three weeks. The more Xu Ning said, the more she felt Chen He did not understand the pressure she was under. The more Chen He heard, the more he felt none of his help counted.

They were not discussing the same thing. Xu Ning was trying to fill a gap in tomorrow's arrangements while also hoping for some understanding. Chen He heard a judgment of him as a person and set about producing counterevidence. The small, urgent matter of collecting photographs had been folded into a much larger issue with no clear boundaries.

## Put Today's Essential Task on the Table First

Xu Ning later started again. “I mixed several things together just now. What's most urgent is who will collect the photographs at two tomorrow afternoon. Can you go? If you can't, I still have time to find someone else today.”

Chen He said, “I can't do two. My class doesn't finish until three thirty. When does the print shop close?”

“Five. But we'd originally arranged for them to arrive at the exhibition space at two.”

“Then we need to ask whether that can change. If four works, I can collect them, but I don't have a car.”

This time, the conversation still did not produce an immediate yes. But both people were now working on the same problem: timing, transport, and alternatives. Xu Ning phoned to confirm that the time could be moved. Chen He would collect the photographs, and another volunteer would meet him outside the shop.

Xu Ning did not conclude that her earlier frustrations had disappeared. She arranged a separate review for the weekend. “After the exhibition, I'd like us all to talk about how we divide the work so we aren't always finding people at the last minute. Today, let's get the collection sorted out.”

Deciding what this conversation needs to do does not mean never discussing feelings. It means giving different problems an appropriate place. When you urgently need to fill a gap, it is difficult to sort out long-standing divisions of labour, mutual judgments, and every old grievance at the same time. Bundling them together often means barely addressing any of them.

## Why Important Matters So Easily Become Huge Topics

Once frustration has built up, people often feel reluctant to discuss only one small, immediate matter. “Who's collecting the photographs tomorrow?” seems inadequate to express days of exhaustion. So we widen the conversation, hoping to say everything at once and make the other person immediately understand how serious it is.

But the other person has not shared the process of sorting it all out in our minds. When they hear “There's a problem with how we work together,” they do not know whether an arrangement needs changing or their reliability needs defending. The less clear the scope, the more they have to guess. If they guess that their whole character is being judged, protecting their image can become the priority.

Sometimes the other person really is avoiding responsibility. Even then, identifying a specific responsibility is easier to check than demanding an admission that they have “always been irresponsible.”

Before speaking, ask yourself: when this conversation ends, what do I want to know that I do not know now, or which arrangement do I want to change? The answer need not be grand. “Find out whether you can deliver a first draft on Friday,” “Clarify what you meant by ‘cancel’ yesterday,” or “Tell you I cannot keep doing this work” are more workable immediate tasks than “Fix the relationship.”

## Ask What the Other Person Thinks the Conversation Is About

Luo Lin, a bakery manager, asked a new employee, Xiao Gu, to stay for a ten-minute conversation after work. As soon as he sat down, Xiao Gu said, “If this is about giving too much change yesterday, I've already paid it back.”

Luo Lin said, “I'm not here to pursue that mistake today. I need to confirm next week's shifts. You said your class timetable was changing, but I haven't received the times.”

Xiao Gu visibly relaxed, then asked, “Will we still talk about yesterday later?”

“Yes. At tomorrow's handover, we'll go through how to double-check the till. Today, we're only scheduling.”

If Luo Lin had dismissed him as oversensitive and said, “You're overthinking it,” Xiao Gu would still not know whether criticism might arrive unexpectedly halfway through the conversation. Explaining today's scope and when the other issue would be addressed made that boundary credible.

When someone's answer does not match your question, do not simply repeat the same words. You can ask, “Which part do you think I'm most interested in discussing right now?” Or state the difference: “You're explaining why it's late, and I do need to know that. But I'm still missing an answer to this: how much can you deliver tonight?”

This is not a test of whether they listened carefully. Your opening may have sent the wrong signal, and it should be open to correction.

## Some Conversations Do Not Need a Solution Yet

A friend, A-Min, had just ended a working partnership and sent a long voice message. After listening to a couple of sentences, Ye Zhou started recommending new collaborators. A-Min said, “I don't want to find someone else straight away.”

Ye Zhou stopped. “Do you want to finish telling me what happened today, or think about what comes next together?”

“Let me finish first. I might want advice tomorrow.”

This too establishes a task for the conversation: today is for helping her work through what happened, not for making an immediate decision. This chapter should not be read as requiring every chat to produce a deliverable like a meeting. Clarifying the task helps you avoid imposing your own on someone else. Their wish to talk also does not mean you must always have unlimited time. Ye Zhou can honestly say he has twenty minutes now and can listen again later.

Sometimes both people genuinely have different tasks. One wants to apologise; the other first needs their materials returned. One wants to explain their intentions; the other needs a refund arrangement confirmed. Time-sensitive matters can come first, followed by understanding and the relationship. There is no need to argue over which need is “more important.”

## How to Return When You Notice You Have Wandered

Conversations often stray. What matters is noticing and adjusting, rather than insisting on saying everything you originally prepared.

You can set aside something already raised: “You mentioned that the last rota was unfair. That needs a separate discussion, and I'll make a note. We have five minutes left. Let's first confirm who opens tomorrow morning.” If the other person thinks the old issue directly affects the new arrangement, ask them to explain the connection: “So if the time off owed from last time hasn't been confirmed, you can't agree to this shift. Is that right?” Then the old issue is not a digression; it is a condition of the new arrangement.

The person who starts the conversation cannot unilaterally fix its limits. You can suggest priorities, and the other person can identify missing conditions. What matters is knowing what you are discussing now and what remains unresolved.

Xu Ning did not end up with a complete reconciliation. Chen He still felt the word “passive” was unfair, and Xu Ning still wanted a better division of work. But the photographs arrived on time, and they had something concrete to discuss at the weekend review. They no longer needed those ten minutes to resolve their entire relationship.

## Reader Exercise

Write down a broad opening you have used recently, such as “We need to have a proper talk” or “Can't you make more of an effort?” Do not send it yet. Rewrite it in two sentences: the first states exactly what you want to discuss today; the second states what answer you hope this conversation will produce.

Then write down a related issue the other person might raise. Decide whether it is a prerequisite that must be addressed today or a topic for another time. If you always put it off until “later,” give it a real time. If you cannot arrange one, do not pretend the issue has been taken care of.

Finally, check: does this conversation allow the answers “I disagree,” “I don't know yet,” or “I can't”? If only one answer is acceptable, at least acknowledge honestly that you are making a demand rather than inviting an open discussion.

# Chapter 2: Make a Request the Other Person Can Answer

Dishes were piled up in the shared kitchen sink again. By the time Tang Yue got home, it was nine thirty. She wanted to cook noodles but could not find a clean pan. She messaged her flatmate: “Could we all take a little more responsibility for the shared space?”

Lin Jia replied, “Sure.” The next day, the pan was clean, but seasonings and rubbish were still on the counter. Tang Yue was angrier. “There's no point saying anything.”

Lin Jia, however, thought he had done what she asked. When he saw the message, he thought of the pan; Tang Yue had the whole kitchen in mind. “Take responsibility” meant different things to each of them. Tang Yue also wanted Lin Jia to do these things without reminders in future, but her actual message did not express that expectation.

## Move from Judgments to Observable Actions

They later talked in the kitchen. Tang Yue began, “When I wanted to cook last night, I couldn't use the pan or the sink. I'd like us to agree that whoever uses the kitchen washes the pots and dishes and clears away food scraps that evening. The seasonings can stay on the counter, but the grease needs wiping off. Do you think that's manageable?”

Lin Jia said, “When I get back from a late shift, I don't always have the energy to do all of that.”

“What time do you usually get home?”

“Sometimes after eleven.”

“Could you wash the pots and dishes first, then? The hob could wait until noon the next day.”

“Yes. But one of the bowls in the sink last night wasn't mine.”

Tang Yue looked and acknowledged that it was her breakfast bowl. They agreed each would deal with their own dishes. Someone getting home late could postpone wiping the hob but could not leave food scraps in the sink.

Making a request specific does not make the requester entirely right. It was precisely because the actions were clear that Lin Jia could identify an error in the scope and explain which parts he could not do. Tang Yue got an answer that required more work than “Sure,” but also an arrangement she could trust more.

## What Requests Most Often Leave Out

We often describe the result we want without saying what we want the other person to do. “I'd like your support” could mean attending together, helping to move things, or not disagreeing in public. Even someone genuinely willing to help may not know where to start.

When turning a result into an action, choose what matters most right now. Rather than “Take more interest in things at home,” discuss “Could you contact the repair technician this Thursday and confirm two possible times for a visit?” Give the action the scope it needs. Do not keep adding conditions so that the other person discovers the cost only after agreeing.

Explain the reason for timing too. Not every request needs a deadline down to the minute. But if the other person does not know when an answer is still useful, you may mistake ordinary consideration for delay. “I need to confirm the list on Friday. Could you let me know by Thursday evening whether you're coming?” is easier to act on than three successive “Are you there?” messages.

Also be clear about what the reply should answer. Are you asking about willingness, ability, or confirmation that something is finished? These are different replies. “Received” cannot automatically be taken to mean “I will deliver on time.”

## A Request Shows Its True Nature When the Answer Is No

A school charity sale needed someone to staff a stall at the weekend. He Wei, the organiser, asked a colleague, “Would you like to help for two hours?”

The colleague said they had plans that day. He Wei immediately replied, “Everyone is doing their bit for the children. Aren't you always saying you care about them?”

The first sentence sounded like an invitation. The second turned refusal into a question of character. Even if the colleague then agreed, it might only be to escape the accusation.

When He Wei later approached another colleague, he explained the actual situation. “We still need someone from ten to twelve on Saturday. If it doesn't work for you, just say so and I'll keep looking. An answer by Thursday is fine.” The colleague could come for only one hour. He Wei accepted that part and covered the remaining hour himself.

This does not mean every responsibility may be freely declined. If you manage the rota and assign a shift within someone's established duties, explain that it is a work assignment and how they can raise difficulties. Do not disguise a requirement that cannot freely be refused as “Could you help if you're free?” and suddenly invoke your authority when they say no.

Clear responsibilities and authority may sound less pleasant than polite formulas, but they reduce mistaken expectations. State which parts are negotiable and which are outside your power to decide.

## Why Hints So Often Lead to Disappointment

Asking directly means facing the possibility of refusal. Hints offer temporary protection: if we never formally ask, it seems we have not really been turned down. We may also believe that someone who cares should think of it without being told.

But hints give the other person the work of interpretation without telling them that work is required. You say, “This box is really heavy,” and they hear a passing comment. You say, “I've been incredibly busy,” and they express sympathy without realising you want them to take over a particular task. When you later get angry, they may feel they have failed an exam whose questions were never supplied.

This does not mean every small matter needs a formal request. Familiar understanding does exist. But when something repeatedly fails to happen, continuing to rely on “They should know” is unlikely to help. Needs should be stated honestly, and refusals should be heard honestly too.

## Offer Choices, Not False Options

A client unexpectedly asked for two extra pages of design. Yao An, the designer, said, “There are two options: keep Friday's delivery and finish the existing six pages, or do eight pages and deliver next Tuesday. Which matters more to you?”

The client asked, “Could you deliver six on Friday and add the other two on Tuesday?”

Yao An checked, found this was feasible, and reconfirmed the price and sequence. Her options helped the client see the trade-offs without pretending she had thought of every possible solution.

A false choice sounds like “Will you take it seriously today, or keep dragging your feet tomorrow?” Both options contain a judgment; neither offers a genuine choice. Another false choice presents an option known to be unworkable simply to make the preferred option look better.

Before offering options, make sure they really fall within your authority and resources. If only one outcome is acceptable, state the minimum requirement honestly, then discuss ways to meet it. There is no need to dress up an already-made decision as a multiple-choice question.

## Reader Exercise

Choose a small request you have not yet made. Write down the specific action, when it needs to be completed, and when you need an answer. Remove words such as “care,” “cooperate,” “take initiative,” and “be serious.” Does the sentence still explain what you want?

Next, write three possible answers for the other person: “I can do it,” “I can do part of it,” and “I can't.” Write your next sentence for each. If the third answer inevitably leads you to attack their character, reconsider whether this is a request, a requirement of their role, or disappointment you have not yet dealt with.

The aim is not to make requests harder to refuse. It is to help both people discover sooner whether an arrangement is possible.

# Chapter 3: When Dividing the Work, Discuss the Handovers Too

At a planning meeting for a small coffee festival, four people quickly divided the work. Lu Chuan would handle the venue, Meng Qing publicity, Wei Da stallholders, and Su Yan overall coordination. Everyone said that was fine.

Two weeks later, the posters were out, but stallholders still did not know when they could arrive. Wei Da assumed the venue side would notify them; Lu Chuan assumed the person in touch with stallholders would send a single notice. Meng Qing spotted the problem first when a stallholder asked the publicity account which entrance to use for parking. She forwarded the question to the group. There was silence for ten minutes, then everyone explained what they had already completed.

The more thoroughly each person described “my part,” the less anyone took on the missing piece.

## Assigning Areas Is Not the Same as Defining Deliverables

“Handle the venue” sounds clear. In fact, it may include inspecting it, signing a usage agreement, collecting keys, checking electricity, drawing a stall plan, and restoring the space afterwards. No one has deliberately lied, but each person may mentally leave out whatever did not occur to them.

Su Yan stopped asking, “Whose responsibility is this?” Instead, she asked everyone to identify the current gap: what stallholders needed to receive, who had that information, and who could send it all together.

Lu Chuan said, “I know about the entrance and power supply, but I don't have all the stallholders' contact details.”

Wei Da said, “I have the list. I can send a notice, but I need a clear entrance map.”

Meng Qing said, “I can put the map together, but I have publicity scheduled this afternoon. I can manage only one revision.”

They made a new agreement: Lu Chuan would provide a venue sketch and entrance photos by one that afternoon; Meng Qing would make a one-page guide by three; Wei Da would check the list and send it by four, then collect the names of stallholders who had not acknowledged receipt. Su Yan would handle any last-minute stalls not already on the list.

They were not trying to document every possible future event. They filled in one actual chain of handovers. Each person knew what to pass on and what the next person would use it for.

## Describe What “Finished” Looks Like

“First draft complete” is a common source of misunderstanding. The writer may mean a complete structure; the recipient may expect copy ready to publish. Both use the same phrase but expect different levels of readiness.

Before handing work over, it helps to use an example to show what “usable” means. A list might need names, telephone numbers, stall categories, and electricity requirements, rather than a screenshot of names alone. A summary sheet should identify missing entries instead of treating blanks as zeros. A design draft may allow photos to be missing temporarily, but unconfirmed dimensions should not be presented as final.

Not every task needs an elaborate spreadsheet. The key questions are: “What will you need to do as soon as you receive this? How far does my work need to have progressed for you to use it?” That grounds the standard in the next person's actual needs rather than the sender's unilateral announcement, “I'm done.”

The recipient also has a responsibility to point out promptly when something does not meet the agreement. Leaving a file untouched for three days and declaring it unusable an hour before the deadline is not solely the sender's problem.

## Shared Responsibility Can Hide Waiting

A brother and sister were helping their parents move. The older sister would arrange movers; the brother would pack. She assumed packing included buying boxes. He assumed the movers would bring them. On Saturday morning, all they had was a few rolls of tape.

“I'll go and buy some now,” the brother said. His sister complained, “Why do we always have to patch things up at the last minute?”

They needed to get boxes, but also to recognise that their earlier arrangement had missed this handover. Later, the sister suggested, “I'll confirm what the moving company provides and list the materials we're missing. You decide what sizes we need and buy them today. Let our parents choose what to do with their personal belongings. We won't throw things away for them.”

The new arrangement also defined decision-making authority. Being responsible for packing does not give someone the right to decide what gets discarded. Being responsible for contacting others does not mean accepting extra charges for everyone. Tasks, decisions, and costs are often bundled into “You're responsible,” with conflict emerging only when money must actually be spent or a decision made.

The person doing the work, the person providing information, and the person making the final decision need not be the same. The more people involved, the more useful it is to distinguish these roles.

## Why We Overestimate How Clear the Division of Work Is

When everyone wants to finish planning, simple role labels are appealing. Each person hears a familiar word and fills in the details from past experience. Discussion seems to have been reduced, but in reality it has merely been postponed until something goes wrong.

There is also a practical concern: asking detailed questions can look petty, and explaining that resources are insufficient can feel like admitting incompetence. So people agree first and try to figure it out alone. Those willing to fill gaps keep doing so, while others may never notice what they have supplied.

Changing this process requires more than telling individuals to ask questions boldly. The coordinator can actively invite people to identify gaps: “It's more useful to tell us now what you can't do than to discover it on the day. Which handovers still have no one responsible?” They then need to make real adjustments, rather than encouraging people to raise difficulties and punishing the first person who does.

## When Conditions Change, Revisit Old Commitments

The coffee festival later added a wet-weather plan. Lu Chuan found an indoor venue, but it could not accommodate all the planned stalls. If he merely posted “We have a backup venue,” others might assume the publicity and list could remain unchanged.

Instead, he explained, “The indoor space holds eighteen stalls, compared with our original twenty-four. Su Yan needs to decide which stalls are affected. Today I can confirm the space, but I can't promise that everyone on the original list can attend.”

Such changes require reopening the agreements they affect. New work not covered by the original division of responsibilities does not automatically belong to whoever notices it first. That person may make necessary temporary arrangements, but should explain that this is temporary help, not a permanent takeover.

Clarifying responsibilities is not about preserving the right to say “I told you so” if things fail. Its value lies in giving others time to adjust. If records are used only to assign blame, people will write defensively and become reluctant to reveal actual progress.

## Reader Exercise

Choose a collaboration currently underway. You do not need to redesign the whole process. Draw one handover: who gives what to whom, what the recipient will do with it, and when they need to discover any problems while there is still time to revise it.

Then circle one decision: when scope, costs, or external commitments are involved, who has final authority? If the answer is “everyone,” add how the decision will be reached and who handles disagreement.

Finally, write a change notice stating which parts of the old agreement still hold and which need reconfirming. Check that you are not treating “I sent a message” as “Everyone accepted the change.”

# Chapter 4: Explaining a Misunderstanding Starts Where Your Understandings Diverged

A pottery studio was replacing some display shelves. Shen Lan, the manager, wrote in the group chat, “Clear out the old shelves on Friday so they don't get in the way of the weekend event.” Her assistant, Xiao Ji, boxed up the pieces on the shelves and asked movers to take the shelves away.

When Shen Lan returned, her expression changed. “I only meant empty them. Why did you throw the shelves away? They were borrowed.”

Xiao Ji showed her the message. “You wrote ‘clear out.’”

“Couldn't you have asked?”

“You didn't make it clear, and now it's all my fault?”

They quickly moved from the shelves to each other's attitude towards work. Shen Lan thought Xiao Ji had acted without authority; Xiao Ji thought Shen Lan was changing her story. The chat record was genuine, but that did not automatically settle how the words should have been understood at the time.

## Explaining Is Not Just Repeating Your Conclusion

At first, Shen Lan kept saying, “I meant take the things off them.” That stated her original intention, but did not explain why Xiao Ji had understood differently. Xiao Ji kept repeating, “You wrote ‘clear out.’” That proved the words existed, not that removing the shelves was the only possible interpretation.

They paused and confirmed that the shelves were still in the movers' temporary storage and could be returned for a fee. Once the immediate damage was contained, they could examine what had happened.

Shen Lan asked, “When you read ‘clear out,’ how far did you think I wanted you to go?”

“Put the pieces away and get rid of the shelves. The day before, you said the old shelves were taking up too much space.”

“So you connected it to that remark too. When I said they took up space, I meant we should move them into the storeroom for the weekend. I didn't tell you they were borrowed. That was something I left out.”

Xiao Ji said, “And I called for a van without checking whether I could dispose of them.”

They had found the point of divergence: one person omitted ownership and destination; the other took “They shouldn't stay here” to mean “You may dispose of them.” That was closer to a fixable problem than arguing about who was more careful.

## Separate Different Kinds of Information

When explaining a misunderstanding, distinguish at least three things: what actually happened, the information available at the time, and guesses about motives.

What happened was that the shelves were taken away. The available information included the original message, the previous day's conversation about space, and the fact that Xiao Ji did not know the shelves belonged to someone else. “You're just shifting the blame” and “You don't care at all” are judgments about motives. They may express real anger, but the outcome alone cannot prove them.

You need not require both people to be free of emotion before talking. You can first acknowledge the impact: “Having the shelves removed did delay you and may add costs.” Then limit yourself to what you can explain: “I can explain why I acted that way at the time. I can't demand that you stop being angry because of it.”

If a mistake occurred, name it. Do not turn “misunderstanding” into a label that removes responsibility. Shen Lan omitted an important condition, and Xiao Ji made a decision beyond what had been confirmed. Understanding the causes does not require finding one entirely innocent person.

## Ask About the Meaning of Words Before Asking About Intentions

Another misunderstanding arose at a reading group. Mi Jia, the organiser, asked the speaker, “Would you be able to send us your talk in advance?” The speaker sent the full text. Mi Jia made promotional cards from two paragraphs. When the speaker saw them, they asked for the cards to be removed.

Mi Jia said, “But didn't you already give it to me?”

The speaker replied, “I thought you needed it to prepare your introduction, not that I was agreeing to publication.”

The issue was not whether the speaker was stingy or Mi Jia had deliberately appropriated someone else's work. “Let the host read it” and “Authorise publication” are different things. Mi Jia removed the cards first, explained how the material had been used, and asked what could be published in future. The speaker approved the event title and a separately written introduction, but not the full text.

Many disagreements conceal similar differences in meaning. “Confirmed” may confirm only receipt. “For reference” does not necessarily permit forwarding. “No problem” may answer a question about dimensions but not delivery dates. Do not expect a familiar phrase to carry every kind of permission. Confirming the specific use is especially important when publication, costs, or irreversible actions are involved.

## Too Much Explanation Can Deepen Distrust

When we feel misunderstood, we often want to provide all the background at once. The more we fear disbelief, the more detail we add. Yet the other person may simply want to know whether we acknowledge the impact already caused.

If Xiao Ji started with the morning traffic, moved on to the movers unexpectedly becoming available, and then described how conscientious he had always been, Shen Lan might feel he was avoiding the issue. More background is not necessarily better. Answer the central difference first, then add information that could change the assessment.

A short sequence may help: what you understand me to have done; what I was acting on at the time; what we now know was different; and how we will handle the consequences. This is not a fixed script, but a way to check for omissions.

Allow the other person to remain unconvinced about your motives. Endless explanation cannot force someone to accept “I meant no harm.” What you can do is offer checkable information, accept established responsibilities, and make subsequent actions consistent with your explanation.

## When You Still Disagree About the Facts

Sometimes there is no chat record and memories differ. You remember saying “before Thursday”; the other person remembers “after Thursday.” Raising your voice will not improve either person's memory.

You can preserve the difference: “We remember the wording differently, and there's nothing available now to establish it. Let's confirm that we currently need it finished by Thursday morning. From now on, we'll write down changes to the date.” This does not mean responsibility can never be addressed; it recognises the limits of the evidence at this moment.

If the dispute affects significant interests and requires records and an appropriate process, do not force it into a private reconciliation. But do not disclose someone else's information on your own initiative to prove your case. Explain what you intend to keep and who will handle it, and limit the material to what is necessary.

Shen Lan and Xiao Ji eventually shared the return transport cost, and the studio introduced a confirmation step for disposing of items. They may not have agreed completely about how to divide the cost, but both knew that “clear out” would no longer be enough to authorise removing borrowed property. The misunderstanding did not become a beautiful moment of mutual understanding. It became a clearer working agreement.

## Reader Exercise

Recall a small misunderstanding that has already ended. Make three columns: checkable events, information you did not have at the time, and motives you guessed. Do not put “They obviously knew” in the facts column unless you can identify evidence for it.

Choose one ambiguous word and write a question you could have asked then. For example: “When you say ‘clear out,’ do you mean move them into storage, or may I arrange recycling?”

Finally, write two explanatory sentences. The first acknowledges an established impact; the second explains the difference in understanding. Check that they do not quietly demand immediate forgiveness or use “I didn't mean to” to erase an actual loss.

# Chapter 5: When You Object, Show Which Step You Disagree With

A bookshop was planning an evening market. Song Jia, the planner, proposed three hands-on activity stalls by the entrance. The manager liked the lively atmosphere and was ready to publish the plan. A-Zhe, who was responsible for on-site operations, looked at the floor plan and said, “This won't work.”

Song Jia looked up. “Which part?”

“You only care about how it looks, not how to make it happen.”

She pushed the plan towards him. “Then you do it.”

A-Zhe had spotted a real problem: the three stalls would block the only delivery route. But his objection turned a layout issue into a judgment of the whole group, so Song Jia defended her work first. The step that actually needed changing had not even been named.

## Mark the Point of Disagreement First

A-Zhe picked up his pen again. “I'm in favour of an activity area by the entrance. What I disagree with is putting all three stalls here at once. Books are still due for delivery on Saturday afternoon, and the van can only come through this passage. If we leave it like this, we'll have to dismantle stalls during the event. I suggest putting one here and the other two in the side room.”

Song Jia said, “Fewer people go into the side room. The stallholders may not agree.”

“That's a valid concern. We could put signs at the entrance or run the activities at different times. My first concern is keeping the delivery route clear.”

The discussion did not automatically end. The manager asked them to confirm the delivery time before deciding whether to change the layout. But at least the objection could now be checked: where the route was, when the van would arrive, and what the alternatives would cost.

A clear objection need not be gentle. When necessary, you can say directly, “I cannot agree to publishing this date.” What matters is identifying the judgment or action you oppose and the reasons for it, rather than leaving someone to guess how their entire character has been assessed.

## Separate Known Facts from Concerns

A-Zhe later called and learned that the delivery van might come in the morning or afternoon. He could no longer present “It will definitely be blocked in the afternoon” as a fact.

He updated the manager. “The delivery time isn't fixed yet. It's a fact that the stalls will occupy the passage, but we don't know whether the delivery will overlap with the market. My concern is that dismantling stalls at short notice will disrupt the event. We could first ask the delivery company to confirm a time. If they can't, we should keep the passage clear.”

This kind of correction does not weaken an honest objection. It shows that the quality of the decision matters more to you than proving you were entirely right at the outset.

You do not need to wait until damage has occurred before raising a concern. But keep the word “may” where it belongs, and explain what information would reduce your concern. If you maintain the same conclusion regardless of new information, others will struggle to tell whether you are reporting a risk or simply dislike the plan.

## Do Not Bury Bad News at the End of the Background

A translation studio had promised a manual by Friday. On Wednesday, the translator discovered that the client's updated version was a third longer. Liang Qing, the project contact, wanted to try to finish and wait until Thursday evening to see whether they could manage.

She worried that reporting early would make her look incapable. But if the client learned of the delay only on the last evening, there would be no time to adjust the release.

Liang Qing sent a message. “We can't deliver the complete expanded version on Friday. We can finish the original scope on time, and expect the additions to be ready on Monday. The extra pages and terminology checks require more time than we planned. I need to tell you about that change today. Which part is most important for you to receive first?”

The client was very unhappy. “Why didn't you say so when you received the material?”

Liang Qing did not deflect with “We're working hard too.” She said, “I failed to reassess it promptly when it arrived. That's my mistake. We've confirmed the shortfall today, so I want to give you the available options first. We also need to reconfirm the fee and final scope.”

Bad news should begin by letting the other person know what has changed as quickly as possible. Explanations can follow, but a long account of your efforts should not obscure the central conclusion. When others need to arrange staff, schedules, or external commitments, earlier knowledge has value in itself.

## Why We Hear Objections as Personal Judgments

A plan often represents considerable effort. When someone says, “This needs changing,” we may hear, “Your previous work has no value.” The person raising the objection may intensify their wording for fear of being ignored. Soon both sides are protecting themselves.

Acknowledging work already done can show that you are not dismissing it. But appreciation should not be a sugar coating followed by sweeping rejection. Specifying what stays and what changes is more credible: “The event theme and publicity copy can stay. I suggest changing only the entrance layout.”

Nor should every objection be labelled emotional defensiveness. The other person may know of constraints you do not, or your suggestion may genuinely increase someone else's costs. Bringing those costs into the discussion is part of addressing the objection, not a detour.

Some teams encourage “thorough debate” without defining who decides or when discussion ends. Task-related disagreement is not inherently beneficial. Going in circles, straining relationships, and consuming time are costs too. The purpose of an objection is to improve judgment, not to prove who dares speak the most.

## You Can Identify a Problem Without Having a Complete Alternative

An intern noticed that two totals in a table intended for publication did not match. She lacked access to all the data and did not know which number was correct. She could still say, “These two figures cannot both be right. I can identify the difference, but I can't currently tell which is correct. Whoever is responsible for the data needs to confirm it before publication.”

Requiring everyone who identifies a problem to provide a complete solution can prevent useful information from surfacing. Explain the problem and possible directions for checking it as well as you can, but do not pretend to have resources you lack.

The person hearing an objection can likewise distinguish three questions: what have you identified, what do you suggest, and who will address it? Someone's lack of an answer does not make the discrepancy disappear.

In the end, the bookshop moved the delivery to before the event, kept two stalls by the entrance, and put the third in the side room. Song Jia still considered the side room less than ideal, and A-Zhe did not get his original layout either. They accepted a decision with explicit trade-offs, rather than a complete victory for one person.

## Reader Exercise

Rewrite “This plan is hopeless” as an objection that states what you would retain, the specific action you oppose, your evidence, and what remains uncertain. If you have no alternative, honestly identify who needs to supply what information.

Then practise delivering bad news: state the change in the first sentence instead of burying the conclusion beneath a description of your efforts. Follow with the confirmed effects and what still needs checking.

Ask yourself: after reading this, would someone know what to check, which step to change, or what decision to make? If all they would know is that you are unhappy, you have not yet addressed the task.

# Chapter 6: In Meetings, Separate Discussion from Decisions

A community reading project held a weekly online meeting. Six people took turns reporting, while the facilitator occasionally said, “That's important” or “Let's give it some more thought.” After forty-five minutes, he concluded, “We'll move forward as discussed.”

After the meeting, Zhou Ning, who handled purchasing, entered fifty sets of materials on the order. He Yin, responsible for recruitment, put the number of places at thirty. Both believed they were following the meeting's decision. Zhou Ning remembered someone proposing fifty participants and nobody objecting. He Yin remembered the project leader saying thirty would be more manageable.

The meeting had discussed both numbers, but at no point had it explicitly chosen one.

## An Option Does Not Become a Decision Just Because It Was Discussed

At the next meeting, the facilitator made the agenda item a question: “Today we need to decide whether the first programme will take thirty or fifty participants, so purchasing and recruitment can use the same number.”

He first explained who had decision-making authority. “Everyone will provide practical constraints, the budget lead will confirm affordable costs, and the project leader will make the final decision about size. If we lack information, we'll identify what's missing today rather than pretending we've decided.”

This did not diminish participants. It told them how their views would enter the decision, without having to compete by volume for an authority no one had defined.

Purchasing explained that fifty sets would bring a lower unit price. The lead teacher said current staffing could support at most thirty participants. The project leader asked about adding volunteers; He Yin explained that training would take at least two weeks. The decision was thirty for the first programme, with expansion considered for the next. Zhou Ning accepted the higher unit price and understood that it was the cost of the staffing constraint.

## Prepare Only Material That Can Affect the Decision

Many meetings run long because people first encounter the information during the meeting. One person brings a budget, another searches for a list, and everyone else waits or speaks from impressions.

There is no need for a thick pre-reading pack every time. A clear question and a few facts needed to decide it may be enough. The reading project circulated the current number of group leaders, the number each group could support, the costs at both purchasing quantities, and the registration deadline. Each item stated when it was updated and who was responsible for checking it.

Anything still uncertain should be identified too. “Volunteer numbers awaiting confirmation” is more useful than an optimistic figure. The meeting should discuss how uncertainty affects the choice, rather than concealing it in an apparently complete table.

If the purpose is simply to inform people of a decision already made, a message with a way to ask questions will often suffice. Do not disguise a briefing as shared decision-making, letting people invest time only to discover that nothing is open to change.

## The Facilitator Needs to Hear Information That Has Not Been Spoken

People remain silent in meetings for many reasons. Some agree, some have not read the material, some feel they have no standing to object, and some know that speaking will not change the outcome. Silence alone does not tell the facilitator which applies.

A facilitator can ask a specific question rather than a general “Any other comments?” For example: “If we buy for fifty participants, where is the group-leading side most likely to fall short?” Or ask each person carrying out the work to write down one constraint before taking turns adding it.

This does not require everyone to speak equally often. Someone with no new information can say so. The aim is to let people who hold essential information enter the discussion, rather than letting the most practised speaker answer for everyone.

More important is how the facilitator responds to bad news. If every difficulty is met with “Stop being so negative,” even a cleverly designed participation exercise may not help next time. A sense of safety in meetings depends on everyday behaviour, not an opening declaration that people should “speak freely.”

## When There Are Many Topics, Explain Why One Is Being Set Aside

At an equipment-purchasing meeting in a small company, finance asked about after-sales costs. A technician suddenly suggested replacing the entire office network too. That might matter, but it was not necessarily a prerequisite for buying printing equipment that day.

The facilitator asked, “Will this machine fail to work properly if we don't replace the network?”

The technician said it would work; they simply hoped to upgrade at the same time. The facilitator recorded the network upgrade for the next budget discussion and assigned someone to gather existing fault records first. Finance returned to after-sales questions.

“Let's set that aside” should not be a polite way of forgetting. If a topic deserves follow-up, specify who will return with what material. If the decision is not to address it, explain why. Catching every suggestion in a list that is never reopened only makes people less willing to speak next time.

Conversely, if the network is necessary for the equipment to work, it cannot be excluded merely to preserve the agenda. The agenda serves the decision. It is not there to let the facilitator read every heading on time.

## What to Say in the Final Five Minutes

As a meeting ends, people are eager to leave. Worried about running over, the facilitator may say only, “Thanks for your work.” Yet this is exactly when a short check is most valuable.

The reading project's summary was: “Thirty participants for the first programme. Purchasing will order thirty sets today. Recruitment will advertise thirty places tomorrow. Even if extra volunteers become available early, this programme will not automatically expand; the leader must confirm that first. He Yin, with this decision in hand, what will you change tomorrow?”

He Yin answered, “I'll cap the registration form at thirty. People on the waiting list won't count as confirmed participants. I'll draft the waiting-list notification for you to review by Friday.”

The question is not a request to repeat a slogan. It checks whether the decision can actually guide action. If the answer reveals a new issue, responsibility for handling it can be assigned on the spot without reopening every detail for debate.

Records should distinguish decisions, action items, and unresolved issues. “Discussed adding volunteers” must not become “Add volunteers.” “Suggested a budget increase” must not become “Budget approved.” Invite corrections after circulating minutes, but do not treat a lack of replies as default agreement to new commitments.

## Do Not Schedule Another Unnecessary Meeting

Zhou Ning realised that whether purchases had arrived could simply be updated in the group chat. There was no need for weekly round-robin reports. The team retained discussions involving trade-offs and moved ordinary updates into short records. Meetings became shorter because not every kind of cooperation was forced into the same format, not because everyone spoke faster.

Some complex issues still require long meetings. The measure is not whether a meeting is as short as possible, but whether the time produces shared judgment, usable arrangements, or a clear next step. Deciding not to act yet can also be an outcome, provided the reasons for waiting and the conditions for reassessment are clear.

## Reader Exercise

Write one answerable main question for your next meeting. State who decides and who holds indispensable information. If you cannot make those clear, do not rush to fill the agenda.

Write sample minutes under three headings: decisions made; who will do what and when; unresolved matters. Check that you have not upgraded a speaker's suggestion into everyone's commitment.

Finally, remove one item that a message could handle, leaving the saved time for disagreements that genuinely need shared judgment.

Relevant sources and limits of application: C01, C04, C05. See [Sources](#sources-and-limits).

# Chapter 7: In Written Messages, Do Not Make the Reader Supply Your Tone and Context

“Take another look at this.”

At ten at night, the illustrator Qiao Yi received a client's message, with the sketch she had sent that morning attached below. She stared at the screen, unsure what needed changing or whether the client was already very dissatisfied.

She replied, “Which part, specifically?”

The client did not answer until noon the following day. “The colours.”

Qiao Yi asked again, “The background or the people? What direction do you want?”

That afternoon, the client chased her: “Why haven't you finished the changes yet?”

Both thought the other was slow to reply. In fact, they had spent several rounds of messages gradually assembling a question that could have been explained in one.

## Before Sending, Consider What the Reader Knows Right Now

The sender has a full context in mind: the meeting just held, the document on screen, the frown or smile accompanying the words. The recipient may see only a sentence and an attachment.

The difficulty of text is not just the absence of a voice. Both people may not be looking at the same page or dealing with the matter at the same time. You say “this,” but they may be handling three versions. You say “like last time,” but they may remember a different discussion.

The client later put the requested changes into one message: “Please revise version 2 of the sketch attached today. Keep the colours of the people, but change the dark blue background to a lighter blue-grey so the title stands out. A preview by noon on Thursday is enough; the details don't need finishing. If that timing won't work, please tell me today.”

The message was longer but reduced necessary guesswork. It specified the object, change, purpose, level of completion, and timing. Not every message needs all of these. But if the other person cannot act without a particular item, it is worth including in advance.

## State the Requested Action Before the Background

Another common type of message is long but unclear. It gives three paragraphs of events, ending only with “What do you think we should do?” After reading, the recipient still does not know whether you want advice, approval, or for them to deal with it.

Put the request at the beginning: “Please confirm whether you agree to moving delivery to Friday. The reasons are below.” The reader immediately knows what they are reading for and what answer is needed.

Keep background that affects the judgment, rather than recording your entire thought process. For a complex situation, start with a short conclusion, attach the material, and identify the part that needs attention. Do not remove conditions essential to the decision merely to appear concise.

Qiao Yi adjusted her reply too. “I can change the background. Do you have a reference for the blue-grey? If not, I'll offer two directions to choose from and send the preview by noon on Thursday.” She did not silently guess a colour and wait for another round of rework.

## Your Tone May Not Sound Like It Does in Your Head

“You're really good at organising things” can be praise or sarcasm. Senders usually know their intended tone and assume readers should hear it too. Research on email has suggested this kind of overconfidence, but it does not directly establish that every WeChat conversation produces the same result.

In practice, a more reliable approach is not to rely on sarcasm, saying the opposite of what you mean, or a single emoji for important arrangements. If you are unhappy, explain which arrangement is problematic. If you are praising, identify what was done well. Friends can of course joke, but once a misunderstanding has occurred, “How can you not understand that?” will not make things clearer.

Nor should every full stop, brief reply, or delay be interpreted as an attitude. The other person may be on the move, have different typing habits, or only have time to acknowledge receipt. Text offers limited clues. When necessary, ask directly about the task rather than interrogating their emotions first.

## A Group Chat Needs an Identifiable Person Responsible

In a class-reunion group chat, someone wrote, “Can someone book a private dining room?” More than a dozen people sent emojis, but by Friday no one had booked.

If an arrangement needs confirming, ask a particular person whether they are willing to take it on, rather than assigning it publicly on their behalf: “Xiao Meng, could you check whether two restaurants have private rooms available? If not, I'll find someone else. Please reply by tonight.” Xiao Meng agreeing to enquire does not mean agreeing to advance a deposit. Costs and the final choice still need separate confirmation.

A group chat is not a perfect record system either. Once a decision is buried under dozens of casual messages, post a separate summary labelled “Confirmed arrangements,” with undecided matters kept apart. Do not keep forwarding an old, long message and make everyone hunt for the changes.

For personal information, criticism, or disputes, first consider whether the group is the right place. Public questioning makes someone face both the task and the audience's judgments. But decisions affecting everyone should not be changed only in private messages either. You can check an individual's circumstances privately, then share the necessary task update with the group.

## When to Switch to a Call

Consider suggesting a short call when written exchanges revolve around “That's not what I meant” rather than the issue itself; when a question has several layers of conditions and each reply creates new ambiguities; or when emotions have both people repeatedly guessing at tone.

“We keep misunderstanding each other's timing in messages. Would you be free for a ten-minute call at eight tonight? I'd like to confirm just the delivery scope.” This respects the other person's schedule more than calling without warning.

A call is not necessarily better. The other person may need text to support understanding, need a record, or find real-time speaking difficult. You can explain by voice and confirm key decisions in writing. Or continue in text while addressing one question at a time. The medium should serve the participants, rather than one person declaring, “If there's an issue, you have to call.”

## When Chasing a Reply, Explain What Happens Next

“Why aren't you replying?” can easily turn a task into a judgment of the relationship. If there is a real deadline, explain the effect of waiting: “I need to submit the list at five today and haven't had your confirmation. If you can't decide in time, I'll leave you off for now. Whether you can be added later will depend on available places.”

What follows must be something you have authority to do and have clearly explained. Silence cannot be taken as permission for charges, publication of information, or new responsibilities. Leaving someone out by default is different from agreeing on their behalf by default.

Qiao Yi eventually delivered the preview using the confirmed colours. The client still changed the position of one title, but this time they were discussing the image, no longer guessing how much dissatisfaction was hidden in “Take another look.”

## Reader Exercise

Find a work or personal message you have not yet sent. Check phrases such as “this,” “that,” “as soon as possible,” “roughly,” and “as before.” Replace any that affect action with a specific object, time, or version.

Then imagine the message appearing on its own, without the background in your head. Can the reader tell whether you want a reply, approval, action, or merely to inform them?

Finally, write a polite, clear follow-up asking for a reply. State the actual deadline and a lawful, appropriate alternative arrangement without turning silence into permission.

# Chapter 8: Discussing Family Time and Money Clearly Without Sounding Cold

Gu Shan wanted to take a two-day woodworking course at the weekend. She sent the course link to her partner, Liang Yue, saying, “I should be allowed some time of my own too, shouldn't I?”

Liang Yue checked the weekend calendar. “Then who's picking up our child? You're assuming I'll handle everything again.”

Gu Shan immediately countered, “Don't I handle it all the rest of the time?”

They had not even reached the course fee before starting to calculate the previous year's contributions. What Gu Shan really wanted was an uninterrupted chance to learn. Liang Yue was worried about work already arranged for Saturday. Both heard the immediate problem as a verdict on long-term fairness.

## Closeness Cannot Replace Concrete Arrangements

Gu Shan started again. “I'd like to enrol in next month's course. It's nine to four on both Saturday and Sunday. The fee fits my own learning budget, but we need to rearrange transport and lunch for those two days. I don't want to sign up first and leave you to sort out the consequences, so let's look at it now.”

Liang Yue said, “Sunday is fine. I have work on Saturday morning, but I can do the afternoon pickup.”

They found that the actual gap was Saturday morning. Gu Shan contacted the course provider and learned she could join the following session. Liang Yue confirmed that work could not be moved. Gu Shan chose the later session rather than giving up the course. They marked the two days in their calendars and agreed to recheck transport once her place was confirmed.

This was not a cost-free outcome. Gu Shan had to wait a month, and Liang Yue would do more household work for two days. But they were discussing actual constraints without first deciding who was more selfish.

Specific arrangements can be mistaken for coldness. Yet “You should understand me” places a considerable burden on a relationship too. Clear times give care a chance to become action rather than a performance of mind-reading.

## Discuss Immediate Arrangements and Long-Term Responsibilities Separately

If Gu Shan had long handled most of the pickups, resolving this course alone would not be enough. She could arrange another discussion: “This enrolment is sorted, but I'm still usually the first person dealing with pickups. Next week, I'd like us to look at how we share them across the month.”

When discussing a long-term division of work, first list the whole job. Cooking includes more than standing at the hob: deciding what to eat, buying ingredients, and knowing what the fridge lacks. Childcare includes more than transport: reading notices, preparing materials, and contacting activity organisers. Invisible work easily disappears from a rota.

But listing work is not meant to create an ever-growing ledger of merit. Its purpose is to show the burden and where changes are possible. People may divide tasks according to time, ability, and preference, rather than splitting each one equally. What needs checking is whether the arrangement has genuinely been accepted and can be revisited when work changes.

If one person is mocked whenever they raise a difficulty, the problem is not merely an insufficiently detailed list. Even the neatest division of chores cannot replace basic respect.

## When Discussing Money, First Establish What Kind of Arrangement It Is

A sister and brother wanted to buy their father a new chair. The sister, Wu Rui, sent a picture. “This one looks good. Shall we give it to him together?” Her brother replied, “Sure.” As she prepared to order, she said he would pay half. Only then did he explain that it exceeded his budget.

“Didn't you say yes?”

“I said the design was fine. I didn't agree to whatever half the price is.”

They had not confirmed the total price, the split, whether delivery was needed, or whether their father actually wanted a new chair. Family closeness made both feel the details need not be discussed. The details then became a source of resentment that was hard to voice.

When they talked again, Wu Rui separated the questions. “The total is 1,800, and I'm willing to pay 1,200. What would you be willing to contribute? It doesn't have to be half. Let's also ask Dad whether it's comfortable. I won't order before that's confirmed.” Her brother could contribute 600. After trying it, their father chose a different, cheaper chair.

There is no single fair cost-sharing formula for every family. What matters is not confusing a gift, a loan, a joint purchase, and a temporary advance in one phrase such as “Just help out for now.” These carry different expectations, which should be clear before action is taken.

## Do Not Use Affection to Extract a Payment

“We're family; why count every penny?” may express closeness, but it can also prevent someone from explaining a real burden. Being unable or unwilling to pay a particular sum does not automatically prove a lack of affection.

You can ask what someone can afford without demanding that they disclose all their income and savings to prove it. The information needed should relate to this arrangement. If you need a firm upper limit, ask for that limit. Do not turn negotiation into a financial interrogation.

Conversely, the person willing to pay more can state a boundary: “I can cover more this time, but that can't mean I'll always make up the difference.” Generosity that depends on unspoken expectations can eventually turn into an unexpected bill delivered in anger.

## Give People Who Are Not Present a Choice Too

Family arrangements often have someone who did not take part in the decision but bears its consequences. Children enrol parents in activities, partners accept dinner invitations for each other, and siblings assume one person will care for an older relative. The initiator may mean well, but good intentions are not permission.

Liang Yue once agreed on Gu Shan's behalf to visit friends on Sunday. “We weren't doing anything that day anyway.” Gu Shan said she wanted to rest. At first, Liang Yue felt embarrassed to change the answer. Later, he acknowledged that a calendar with no events does not mean someone's time is waiting for others to use.

He told the friends he had not yet checked and reopened the arrangement, rather than making Gu Shan the villain who refused the gathering. Whoever first made an unconfirmed commitment should do their best to take responsibility for correcting it.

## A Trial Is Easier to Adjust Than a Permanent Settlement

Gu Shan and Liang Yue later assigned Tuesday and Thursday pickups to Liang Yue for an initial two-week trial. In the second week, they found his Thursday meetings often ran over, so they switched that day to Wednesday. An initial arrangement failing does not prove unwillingness to help. Look at the specific point where it breaks down.

A trial cannot become endless delay either. Specify when to review it, what to judge it by, and who is responsible during the trial. If every conversation ends with “Let's see how it goes,” the actual burden may remain with the same person as before.

## Reader Exercise

Choose a forthcoming family arrangement and write down its timing, costs, tasks, and decision-making authority. In particular, identify anyone who may be affected without having joined the discussion, and leave them a chance to confirm or decline.

Split “You never consider me” into two matters: an arrangement that needs changing this week and a long-term issue to discuss separately. Do not pretend resolving the first makes the second disappear.

Finally, write a trial division of work and agree on a review date. Check whether “shared responsibility” still means only one person remembering, reminding, and filling the gaps.

Relevant source and limits of application: C07. See [Sources](#sources-and-limits).

# Chapter 9: Turn Dissatisfaction with a Service into Something That Can Be Addressed

Su Rong ordered a desk with drawers. After delivery, the installer found that parts for a drawer runner were missing. He assembled the desk, left a customer-service number, and went away. Su Rong contacted the seller the next day. Customer service said they would pass it on, but three days later there was still no news.

By the next contact, she no longer wanted to explain calmly. “Does anyone there actually do any work? I didn't pay to be put through this.”

Customer service replied, “Dear customer, we completely understand how you feel. Please be patient.”

That made her angrier. She needed a usable desk, not a repeated expression of sympathy.

## First Specify What You Want Addressed

Su Rong condensed the history into one paragraph. “The desk installed on Saturday is missing a set of drawer runners, so the drawer can't be used. I contacted you on Sunday and Tuesday and still have no date for the parts. First, I'd like to confirm whether the parts are in stock and the earliest date for an installation visit. Please don't reply only that it has been passed on.”

Customer service checked stock and said the parts could be sent, but another team had to arrange installation. Su Rong asked, “Then please tell me separately: when will the parts be sent, who will contact me about installation, and when can I expect an answer?”

She did not get an immediate solution. One customer-service representative could not control every stage. But the issue was no longer simply “the customer is angry.” It had become several specific matters that could be referred to the relevant people.

You need not suppress every emotion when complaining, nor perform extreme anger to prove the seriousness of a problem. You can explain clearly that use has been affected and provide enough information to identify the order and issue. Telling someone whether you want a repair, replacement, cancellation, or explanation usually gives them more to work with than demanding that they “give you an answer.”

## Record Events Without Enlarging the Conclusions

One installation error justifies a complaint about that installation. It does not establish that every employee deceives customers. Separating checkable facts from assumptions is not only polite; it also reduces irrelevant disputes.

Su Rong kept photographs of the missing part, the installation date, and records of previous contacts. She did not post the representative's personal information in a public group or threaten to disclose unrelated material. When escalation was needed, she submitted the problem, the contact history, the answers still missing, and the matters she wanted resolved.

A record should help the next person pick up the case quickly, rather than burying them in an unedited pile of chats. Ten screenshots can be attachments, but they still need three or four sentences of summary in front. Otherwise, the new handler may ask you to start again, leaving both sides more frustrated.

Also check whether your request rests on the original agreement. Did the product description include installation? Which service was explicitly promised, and what differed in practice? If something was never promised, you can negotiate adding it, but do not present a new request as something already owed.

## Finding Someone with Authority Does Not Require Attacking the Person in Front of You

A print shop bound conference materials in the wrong page order. The assistant offered to reprint them but could promise collection only the following afternoon. The customer, Chen Rong, needed them that evening and asked for immediate action.

The assistant said, “Today's machines are fully scheduled. I can't change the order.”

Chen Rong asked, “Who can decide whether to fit in the reprint or use another machine? I'm happy to explain the problem directly. Please help me contact them. I don't need you to promise something you can't do.”

The manager arrived and offered to reprint only the twenty copies essential that day, with the remainder the next day. Chen Rong had wanted forty, but the organiser confirmed that twenty would allow people to share in pairs. He accepted the partial remedy while retaining the arrangement for the remaining materials.

Understanding authority is more useful than repeatedly demanding that the same person “take full responsibility.” The employee in front of you should make the referrals and explanations within their role, but may not be able to decide refunds, scheduling, or compensation. Escalation should seek the authority to handle the matter, not use humiliation to prove you are right.

If the person refuses to refer the issue and cannot offer a trackable process, you may consider the relevant platform or formal channel. Rules vary by location and service type. This book does not determine readers' specific rights or deadlines. When necessary, check the applicable rules rather than copying a generic script.

## Before Accepting a Remedy, Ask About All Its Costs

Su Rong later received an offer: the seller would send the parts for her to install herself and refund part of the installation fee. She did not immediately agree, because she had no tools and was unsure she could install them correctly.

She asked, “If I don't install them myself, how will the original installation service be completed? Once the parts arrive, who will confirm the visit time?” Customer service eventually offered a bookable slot the following Wednesday.

That was later than she wanted. Su Rong needed to decide whether the wait was acceptable, rather than being pushed to close the case by “We've already offered you a solution.” She booked the visit and asked them to confirm that sending the parts and returning would involve no extra charges, and who would follow up if the parts did not fit.

A proposed remedy does not mean the problem is solved merely because it exists. It may transfer costs to you: another day off, moving something yourself, or advancing a payment. You may accept those trade-offs, but should decide with the costs in view.

## Pleasant Service Does Not Necessarily Mean Progress

A friendly representative can make you feel awkward about pressing for answers. A rude one can make you want only to win the argument. Both can draw attention away from actual progress.

You can assess the two things separately: “Thank you for checking that. The installation date still isn't confirmed, and I need to know who is handling that step.” If the tone is brusque, you can return to the task: “Let's first clarify how the order will be handled.” If there is abuse or a threat, you have no obligation to remain in the same conversation.

Likewise, if you said too much in anger, you can withdraw an attack without withdrawing a reasonable request. “I said earlier that all of you were lying. That wasn't accurate. I still need the missing parts and installation delay resolved.” Retracting an unsupported generalisation is not surrender. It puts the complaint back on a basis that can be checked.

## How to Know When This Follow-Up Can End

The desk was finally completed the following Wednesday. Su Rong tested the drawer on the spot, confirmed that it opened and closed properly, and only then told customer service the installation was complete. She had not treated “dispatched” as resolution, and she did not continue asking about the same matter every day once it was finished.

A case can have different endpoints: the fault is fixed, both sides accept an alternative, a clear refusal leads to another channel, or you decide to invest no more time. The last choice does not necessarily mean the complaint was unjustified. It means the cost of pursuing it matters too.

## Reader Exercise

Describe a service problem in five sentences: the original agreement, the actual difference, contacts already made, the remedy you want, and the answer needed next. Remove judgments of character and unrelated information.

Then compare two remedies, considering not just money but waiting time, your additional work, and whether normal use is genuinely restored. Write down the most important point to confirm before accepting.

Finally, define when this follow-up will end. Avoid confusing “They finally apologised” with “The problem has been addressed.”

# Chapter 10: Speaking to Someone Who Has Power over Your Situation

Lin Chen, a new employee, was compiling an external briefing. Just before a meeting ended, his supervisor said, “Use last year's basis for the customer figures and send it out today.” Lin Chen noticed that the scope of one measure had changed this year, so copying the previous approach could mislead readers.

He did not object in the meeting. The week before, another colleague had raised a different view and received the reply, “Just do it. Don't make a simple thing complicated.” Lin Chen did not know whether another question would make him seem difficult to manage.

From outside, this might look like something that needs only a clearly worded sentence. But Lin Chen was facing more than someone with a different opinion. This person assigned his work and evaluated his performance. The consequences of speaking were not equal.

## First Assess How Quickly the Problem Needs Attention

Lin Chen checked the briefing: two hours remained before sending, and the supervisor had not told him to conceal the change in scope. He put both measurement bases on one page and sent it privately. “One point needs confirmation before release. This year's figures include two additional channels. Using last year's heading may make clients think the numbers are directly comparable. We could keep the figures and add a note about the scope, or leave this item out for now. Which would you prefer?”

The supervisor initially replied, “We've always done it this way.”

Instead of arguing that “the past isn't the present,” Lin Chen located the difference. “Those two channels weren't included last year, so the heading was valid then. This is the item that has changed this year. I'm concerned that we may give inconsistent explanations if clients ask.”

The supervisor eventually agreed to add the note, but told Lin Chen not to widen the discussion. Lin Chen had not achieved a candid review of management practices, but the briefing was corrected. He understood that this was a result for the current task, not proof that future questions would carry no risk.

When there is immediate serious danger, waiting for a more comfortable conversational moment may be inappropriate. Ordinary work disagreements, however, do not always require a public challenge. The key considerations are urgency, impact, and available channels, not the idea that “being brave means speaking up on the spot.”

## Private Is Not Always Better, and Public Is Not Always Braver

Private communication can reduce pressure from an audience, but may also leave a problem without a necessary record. Speaking publicly can inform relevant people promptly, but may increase the personal consequences.

When choosing a channel, consider who needs to know, who has authority to act, and whether a credible formal process exists. Not every organisation has a safe, effective escalation route. “Go to the next level” is no guarantee of protection. If your information is confidential or personal, you also need to limit who receives it.

You can begin with the smallest adequate way of raising the issue: identify the difference, its effect, and the decision needed. Where necessary, keep task records you are entitled to hold. Do not obtain or circulate material you have no right to access merely to prove your position.

This caution is not advice to endure everything silently. It recognises real costs. Choosing not to speak for now, using another channel, or seeking support should not automatically be judged a failure of responsibility.

## Ask for Priorities Rather Than Treating Unlimited Work as a Test of Ability

He Lin, an administrative assistant, received two urgent assignments one morning. The department manager wanted changes to a meeting venue completed by noon. Another lead wanted a set of contract documents organised by eleven. Both insisted their task was most urgent.

He Lin initially planned to work faster without saying anything, then realised no amount of rushing would finish both. She told her direct supervisor, “I have two conflicting deadlines. The venue changes need about two hours, and checking the documents also needs two. There are three hours left this morning. Please decide which comes first or arrange for a colleague to share the work. I can start immediately, but I can't promise both by their original deadlines.”

The supervisor said, “Coordinate it yourself.”

He Lin followed up. “I can contact both sides about changing the times, but both are insisting on the original deadlines. If the conflict remains, should I put the venue first and deliver the documents at one?”

This time she proposed a definite provisional order for the person with authority to confirm, rather than continuing to accept an overall demand unsupported by resources. The supervisor agreed to contact the other lead, and the documents were postponed.

Sometimes a supervisor still refuses to decide. You can truthfully report your plan and what you cannot complete, avoiding false promises. That does not guarantee freedom from criticism. When a system pushes conflicting demands onto the person doing the work, communication can expose the conflict; it cannot create extra working hours.

## Uncertainty Does Not Need to Become a Moral Accusation

When speaking upwards, nervousness can send us to either extreme: making the problem almost inaudible or jumping straight to “This is completely irresponsible.” The first prevents the decision-maker from judging its seriousness. The second may create another conflict without sufficient evidence.

Put the force in the facts and consequences. “This table is currently missing twelve entries, so it cannot be sent today as a complete list” is clearer than “There may be a tiny issue,” and easier to address than “You never respect data.”

If it is a concern, call it a concern. If it is confirmed, say so. If it involves something you cannot do, state your limit. You need not sacrifice information to vague politeness or add weight through accusations about motives.

## What Those with Authority Need to Do

This chapter should not merely ask newcomers like Lin Chen to learn better techniques. Managers who want to hear the truth have responsibilities too.

When staff report bad news, check the task before immediately questioning their attitude. Explain which matters they may decide and which they must escalate. Address a real problem someone identifies even when they have no complete solution. Most importantly, do not later punish ordinary questions through shifts, evaluations, or ridicule.

One shop manager asked at a handover meeting, “You can raise any problems.” When an employee reported an abnormal refrigerator temperature, the manager complained that they were spoiling the mood before opening. Later, the manager changed approach: first record the abnormality and arrange an inspection, then review why it had not been noticed earlier. Only repeated behaviour of that kind can begin to change people's expectations of what happens when they speak.

## When the Environment Does Not Change

Lin Chen was still interrupted on later occasions. Instead of attributing every failure to poor wording, he observed which problems could be addressed, which were simply pushed back, and whether useful support existed. He could use that knowledge to decide how to communicate and, when circumstances allowed, whether to change his working situation.

This book will not decide for readers whether to endure, leave, or formally escalate. Those choices involve income, responsibilities, and actual safety. The judgment to retain is this: communication skills are your tools, not an excuse that absolves others of the consequences they impose on you.

## Reader Exercise

Choose a low-risk work disagreement. Write down who has decision-making power, who will bear the consequences of an error, and who genuinely needs to know. Do not apply this exercise directly to a dangerous situation.

Prepare a brief explanation including the facts, impact, decision needed, and timing. Then describe a realistic arrangement if the other person does not respond, distinguishing what you may do yourself from what requires authorisation.

If you manage others, recall the last time someone brought you bad news. What did you ask first? Do they have reason to believe that telling the truth next time will not bring an additional penalty?

# Chapter 11: When Agreement Is Out of Reach, You Do Not Have to Repeat the Conversation Forever

Two friends ran weekend craft classes together. Yu Man taught; Zhou Heng handled recruitment and the venue. Enrolment was below expectations. Zhou Heng wanted to turn the course into a two-hour taster session, reducing venue costs and the price. Yu Man felt two hours could not accommodate the original design and would agree only to removing one activity.

They had already discussed it three times. Each began with “Let's communicate properly this time” and ended where they had started. Yu Man felt Zhou Heng cared only about revenue; Zhou Heng felt Yu Man did not understand costs.

For the fourth conversation, Yu Man brought a more detailed teaching outline and Zhou Heng a more detailed cost sheet. Both sets of information were fuller. The disagreement remained.

## Decide Whether You Are Stuck on Information or on a Choice

They finally wrote the problem in two sentences. Yu Man would not offer, under the current brand, a course she regarded as incomplete. Zhou Heng could not absorb the expected loss at the original duration and price. Both limits were real and would not change merely through another explanation.

If information is missing, add it. If understanding is the problem, explain differently. But if both people understand and make different choices about costs and limits, you cannot keep calling disagreement “You didn't understand.”

Zhou Heng said, “I understand that you don't want to cut it to two hours, and I know why. I still can't rent the venue for the original plan.”

Yu Man replied, “And I know you're not trying to cut teaching time just for the sake of it. Then we need to decide whether to skip this session.”

These sentences did not resolve the tension between revenue and quality. They ended the cycle in which each person first had to admit that the other's values were wrong.

## What Would Make Another Conversation Worthwhile?

Continuing is not always a waste. A new option, new information, the participation of someone with decision-making power, or time for both sides to reconsider may justify another conversation.

But explain why the next one might be different. Without new material or changed conditions, “Let's talk again later” can simply postpone refusal.

Yu Man suggested a smaller venue, and Zhou Heng agreed to check prices at two places. They agreed that if no venue within budget was found by Wednesday, they would cancel this session and stop recruiting. The deadline came from venue and enrolment arrangements, not an arbitrary countdown intended to force someone to give in.

On Wednesday, neither venue was suitable. They cancelled as agreed, explained the arrangement to people already enrolled, and divided responsibility for refunds and notifications. Neither had been persuaded to accept the original proposal, but the partnership did not continue draining away through endless accusations.

## What Needs Explaining When You Pause?

A pause may help when emotions rise, but “I don't want to talk anymore” can leave the other person unsure whether the matter has been abandoned.

In an ordinary disagreement that is safe and appropriate to continue addressing, you can specify how long the pause will last and the temporary arrangements: “We're both repeating ourselves. I'd like to pause until tomorrow evening. We won't place an order or promise anyone a date today. Tomorrow, let's discuss only the two workable options.”

The other person need not accept all your reasons for you to stop taking part in this round of conversation. But a pause does not let you unilaterally change responsibilities already accepted. If you have promised to care for someone or something, arrange a handover first rather than using silence to leave the costs with others.

Where there are threats or real danger, leaving does not depend on promising to return. Agreements suited to ordinary disagreements must not be used to keep someone in an unsafe situation.

## What Do You Want a Third Party to Do?

Two residents argued over a communal storeroom. One said they had used its corner for years. The other said personal belongings should never have been stored there long-term. Both explained their difficulties, but made no progress.

Continuing to argue about who deserved more sympathy could not establish the rules for communal space. They needed management to explain the applicable arrangements and give affected residents a chance to raise practical difficulties.

Before involving a third party, specify what you want them to do: check facts, explain rules, facilitate discussion, or make a decision within their authority. Do not call it mediation while actually recruiting friends to prove the other person is bad.

A third party is not necessarily neutral or empowered. Friends may know only one side; management may not be able to decide everything. Choose a channel by its actual responsibilities and procedures, rather than treating “someone stepping in” as a guarantee of resolution.

Limit the material to what is needed. Widely circulating private chats to gain support may create further harm without making the original issue easier to resolve.

## End a Collaboration with a Clear Close

Yu Man and Zhou Heng later decided not to continue running classes together for the time being. They had different basic views of what the course should be and were unlikely to agree soon. This did not erase the two earlier sessions they had delivered together, nor did friendship require them to continue working together.

As they closed the project, they checked each unfinished matter: who would notify students, who would keep remaining materials, how to reconcile costs already paid, and when the publicity page would stop taking registrations. One cost-sharing dispute remained. They listed it separately and kept checking earlier records, without letting it block completion of undisputed matters.

Ending a collaboration easily falls into two extremes: saying nothing to preserve appearances and leaving money and materials unattended, or turning every small matter into a chance to punish the other person. A clear close aims to keep existing responsibilities from disappearing with changing emotions.

Whether to continue the personal relationship can be decided separately. Ending a business arrangement need not automatically become a verdict on the entire relationship.

## Accept That You Did Not Get the Answer You Wanted

Failure to agree is usually disappointing. You may have been restrained, well prepared, and reasonable, and still received a refusal. It is tempting to search for a more powerful sentence, as if one more communication technique would change the result.

But the other person has different preferences, resources, and rights. Clear conversation can reduce misunderstanding; it cannot guarantee that differences disappear. Accepting this makes it possible to direct energy towards arrangements you can actually make.

Yu Man still regretted not running the course. Zhou Heng still felt cancellation had wasted publicity effort. They did not package that regret as a win-win. They simply stopped extending it through another identical argument.

## Reader Exercise

Choose a small recurring disagreement with no real danger. Write down whether both people already understand each other's requests. If they do, list the costs that are genuinely unacceptable.

Then ask: what would the next conversation need to add to produce a different result? If the answer is only “I hope they finally come round,” consider adding information, finding alternatives, involving an appropriate third party, or acknowledging that agreement is currently out of reach.

Write a closing checklist containing actual responsibilities, not judgments of character. Separate undisputed matters from disputed ones so one disagreement does not hold up everything that follows.

# Chapter 12: After the Conversation, Connect Agreements to Action

On Sunday evening, four friends discussed holding a second-hand swap event. The conversation went smoothly, and they provisionally chose the second Saturday of the following month. Someone would handle the poster, someone could borrow tables, and another person would ask about a venue. Everyone left feeling the project was underway.

Two weeks later, the poster framework was ready, but no date or address had been confirmed. The person responsible for the venue was still waiting for an acquaintance to have time before asking. The person borrowing tables assumed there was no need to contact anyone until the venue was confirmed. Nobody had deliberately broken a promise, yet nothing was moving.

A smooth conversation had given them shared enthusiasm, but not all the conditions needed to begin.

## End by Saying What Each Person Will Do Next

Lin Yuan, who had initiated the event, brought everyone together again. Instead of first asking who had delayed progress, he asked, “For each of the things we discussed last time, what's the next step?”

The poster designer said they had expected a date that same week. The venue contact said they had only promised to ask a friend, not guaranteed they could borrow the space. The person arranging tables said they needed to know whether the event was indoors or outdoors before choosing the kind of table.

They realised that several statements sounding like commitments contained different degrees of certainty. “I can ask” meant attempting contact, not guaranteeing a result. “I'll handle the poster” depended on a location and date. “I'll sort something out for tables” did not even specify a number.

This time they made the first step smaller: confirm venue availability by Tuesday evening; if unavailable, Lin Yuan would enquire about a public activity room; decide by Thursday whether to proceed; once the date and address were confirmed, the poster designer would provide a publishable version within two days. They would not borrow tables yet. First, the venue contact would check how many were already available.

The agreement had not grown more elaborate. It had removed work that could not yet begin.

## Link Commitments to a Trigger

“Keep people informed promptly in future” expresses a wish but does not say when to notify them. “When you realise you can't deliver on Thursday, tell the recipient that day rather than waiting until Thursday” is closer to an actionable arrangement.

Linking action to a specific occasion can reduce the burden of deciding afresh in the moment. But a plan cannot replace resources or commit someone else. It becomes a person's arrangement only when they understand and accept it.

Lin Yuan and the venue contact agreed, “Even if there's no reply by Tuesday evening, post ‘unconfirmed’ in the group. We'll start checking the backup instead of quietly waiting.” The trigger was not “when it feels like too long,” but a time both knew.

Not everything needs this precision. Small matters in stable everyday cooperation can remain flexible. What deserves clarity is a point where delay affects others or where things have often stalled before.

## Make Changes Easy to Find in the Record

The planning group received new messages every day. Lin Yuan put confirmed information into a short record: the currently planned date, venue status, each person's next step, and unresolved questions. Each revision highlighted only what changed, rather than resending a large block of unmarked text.

He did not turn “Suggest inviting neighbours” into “Each person invites five neighbours,” or use “We'll proceed unless anyone objects” to create responsibilities others had never accepted. If the record differed from people's actual understanding, he checked again rather than using the minutes as evidence to overrule them.

A record gives cooperation a shared reference. It is not a tool for policing people with poor memories, nor is its only purpose to say later, “Look, you promised.” If everyone knows records exist only to assign blame, they may avoid writing down real commitments in the first place.

## When Following Up, Ask About the Actual State of Progress

On Tuesday evening, the venue was still unconfirmed. Lin Yuan did not send, “Why isn't it sorted yet?” He asked, “Have you contacted them and are waiting for an answer, or have you not reached anyone yet? We need to decide tonight whether to check the backup.”

Those states call for different responses. The first may require a different contact or an end to waiting. The second may mean work has not started, requiring an understanding of obstacles and a revised assignment. “Is it done?” is brief, but often gets only “Not yet.”

The venue contact admitted that they had kept waiting for the acquaintance to be free and had not formally stated the timing needed. Lin Yuan was disappointed, but first checked the backup as agreed. Later, they discussed why the task had not started on time and whether this person should handle it in future.

Following up does not mean filling every gap for someone else. You can help manage an immediate risk while retaining a discussion about responsibility. Otherwise, whoever first spots a problem continually takes over, and the original division of work exists only in name.

## When an Agreement Changes, Check Again with Those Affected

The public activity room was finally available, but only the day before the original date. Lin Yuan could not announce that everyone agreed to reschedule just because he was free. He first asked the two friends responsible for running the event whether they could attend, then decided whether further planning was worthwhile.

One could not attend. The group reduced the event, dropping on-site repairs and retaining only the swap. The poster needed changing, and those already invited verbally needed to be told again. Changing a date affects more than one number on a calendar.

Stating which old agreements no longer apply and which remain can prevent people from continuing with an outdated version. Arrangements already announced externally especially need a named person to correct them. A single “It's changed” in an internal group is not enough.

## When Results Disappoint, Keep a Fact You Can Learn From

After the event, the tables had been sufficient and the poster had gone out on time, but registration had briefly become crowded. At the review, it would have been easy to conclude, “We'll coordinate better next time.”

Lin Yuan asked, “At which step did the crowding first begin?” They found that attendee check-in and item registration shared a table, while item details also had to be handwritten on arrival. Next time, they could collect basic information in advance and separate the two stations.

This kind of review focuses on a specific process rather than rushing to judge who was keen or lazy. It need not turn every mistake into ten new rules. Identifying one recurring or high-impact step, changing it, and seeing whether that helps is usually more useful than writing a long report no one opens again.

## Clarity Is Cooperation That Can Be Corrected

Across these twelve chapters, we have kept doing a few simple things: knowing what this conversation is about, making requests and objections answerable, correcting differences in understanding promptly, and confirming decisions separately from follow-up responsibilities.

These practices have not removed emotion or given every story a perfect ending. People remain disappointed, resources remain limited, and collaborations sometimes end. But matters need not always remain stuck between “You should know,” “That's not what I meant,” and “Let's talk later.”

Speaking clearly does not require having every answer before you begin. It asks you to let missing information, actual limits, and existing disagreements become visible, and to let others correct your understanding.

Sometimes the most useful next sentence is short: “I wasn't precise enough just now. Let me check that again.” Only then does the matter have a real chance to move forward.

## Reader Exercise

Choose an arrangement already agreed verbally. Write down the next action, who will do it, the necessary conditions, and when they should notify someone if it cannot be done. Check whether these points were agreed by both sides or added by you alone. The latter need another discussion.

Write a follow-up message asking about the specific state of progress and explaining why you need to know now. Avoid automatically translating “unfinished” into “You don't care.”

Finally, define the end of the arrangement: handing over a file, the recipient confirming it is usable, completing a refund, or explicitly cancelling. Once the endpoint is reached, stop unnecessary tracking and leave your attention for the next matter that genuinely needs it.

**What this book draws on:** Expressing requests clearly, checking whether refusal is genuinely allowed, and avoiding the use of guilt to compel cooperation.

**This book's limits:** It does not require a complete formula in every conversation, explain all behaviour through a single need, or demand that people stay indefinitely in dangerous or ineffective conversations. The task structures, scenes, and dialogue in Chapters 2, 8, and 11 are original. This is not an NVC-certified textbook or a substitute for NVC publications.
